# Teaching WH-questions (especially "who") to autistic children, evidence on tablet/app instruction, and autism-friendly app design: what it means for building a "who" app

Scope note: this covers teaching procedures, naturalistic approaches, tablet/app evidence, design and privacy guidance, and design implications. It does not cover paediatric screen-time guidelines or competitor apps, which other researchers are handling. Evidence grading used throughout:
- **STRONG**: an RCT, a group meta-analysis, or a national systematic review.
- **MODERATE**: several replicated single-case studies, or a single-case meta-analysis.
- **WEAK**: one single-case study, a thesis, a secondary summary or expert opinion.

Many findings below come from abstracts or secondary summaries because the full text was paywalled. These are flagged.

---

## 1. What do peer-reviewed studies show for teaching autistic children to answer WH-questions?

### Takeaway
WH-question answering is reliably taught with behavioural procedures. The building blocks are:
- visual or picture prompts, plus echoic prompts that model only the answer;
- systematic prompt fading, such as constant or progressive time delay;
- differential reinforcement;
- multiple-exemplar training.

Generalisation to new questions, new WH-words and new settings is the weak point. It usually has to be programmed explicitly, for example by mixing WH-types, using many exemplars and probing in natural settings. Nearly all of this evidence is single-case research (MODERATE). No RCT exists on WH-question teaching specifically.

### Cited Findings

**Foundational WH-question studies**
- **Secan, Egel & Tilley (1989, JABA 22(2):181–196).** The study taught autistic students generalised answers to *what*, *how* and *why* questions. The abstract does not list *who*. It used modelling and reinforcement with magazine pictures as referents, and split each question form into subcomponents reflecting common social usage. Picture training produced generalised responding *when the relevant cue was visible*. When the cue was not visible, specific generalisation programming was required. All acquired responses were durable over time (MODERATE). — [PMC1286168 / DOI 10.1901/jaba.1989.22-181](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1286168/)
- **Secan et al. (1989), as described by a later paper.** A 2015 *Research in Autism Spectrum Disorders* paper says the study "examined the use of visual prompts to increase correct responding to wh- questions". It adds that visual prompts of different colours helped individuals answer correctly. This is the origin of the "colour-coded WH visual cue" idea. It is a secondary description and the details were not verified. — [Derived emergence of WH question–answers in children with autism (RASD 2015)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1750946715000677)
- **Jahr (2001, *Research in Developmental Disabilities* 22(5):407–423).** Five autistic children were trained with multiple exemplars sharing a common sentence frame, and full-sentence answers were required. All children came to answer *novel* questions within each trained WH-class. These skills transferred across persons, settings and time. This comes from a secondary summary; the full text was not read (MODERATE). — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222874784_Teaching_children_with_autism_to_answer_novel_wh-questions_by_utilizing_a_multiple_exemplar_strategy); [summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/teaching-children-with-autism-to-answer-novel-wh-questions-by-utilizin/)
- **Barthold (2007, University of Maryland dissertation, chaired by Egel).** Six students were taught WH-questions. Only two of them generalised to novel WH-questions after training (WEAK, unpublished). This shows generalisation cannot be assumed. — [UMD dissertation PDF](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/bitstream/1903/6833/1/umi-umd-4321.pdf)
- **iPad-based AAC study (*International Technology and Education Journal*, 2025).** A single-case multiple-baseline study covered *what*, *where* and *who* questions with a 15-year-old autistic student. The package combined:
  - a least-to-most prompting hierarchy;
  - interval reinforcement;
  - WH cue cards and question-related visuals.

  Unprompted correct answers stayed high for all three WH-types at follow-up (WEAK, n=1). — [ITEJ 2025 PDF](https://www.itejournal.com/articles/effects-of-an-ipad-based-augmentative-and-alternative-communication-intervention-on-wh-question-answering-in-an-adolescent-with-autism.pdf)
- **JMU master's thesis.** It tested a differential observing response (DOR) with a **two-stimulus visual array** on independent answering of *what/where/who* questions by a child with autism (WEAK, n=1). — [JMU Scholarly Commons](https://commons.lib.jmu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1395&context=masters202029)
- **Pearson EBP Brief on language disorders.** Few studies have analysed interventions for WH-question answering, and no two studies targeted the same WH-questions. Most participants had ASD or hearing loss. — [SpeechPathology.com – Pearson EBP Brief](https://www.speechpathology.com/articles/wh-question-intervention-for-children-20273)
- **Raulston et al. (2013, RASD) on question *asking*.** The review covered 21 studies, and all reported acquisition. Common components were contriving motivation, environmental arrangement, preferred items as reinforcers and systematic prompting with fading. Generalisation was mixed. In one study, generalisation across question types (e.g., *who* to *what*) did not occur. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1750946713000597); [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/a209bac4-3b89-4115-bb3d-5c15a45cce6d)

**Developmental context for "who"/"what"**
- **Goodwin, Fein & Naigles (2012, *Autism Research* 5(2):109–123).** WH-question comprehension precedes production in both autism and typical development.
  - Typically developing children comprehended subject-wh and object-wh questions by about 29 months.
  - The autistic group (n=15) only showed significant group-level comprehension at about 54 months, when their language level (MLU about 2) matched the typical children's.
  - Object-wh comprehension was clearer than subject-wh at that visit (subject p = .07).
  - The comprehension task used *what* questions ("What hit the flower?"). *Who* appeared only in production data.

  Children with higher language levels showed better WH-comprehension. — [PMC3329597 / DOI 10.1002/aur.1220](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3329597)
- **Jyotishi, Fein & Naigles (2017).** Word-order (grammar) knowledge and social-pragmatic scores each independently predicted later WH-question comprehension in both groups. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00319/pdf)

**Prompt types (echoic vs visual/picture vs textual)**
- **Ingvarsson & Hollobaugh (2011, JABA 44(2):659–664).** Three 4-year-old autistic boys were taught intraverbal (question-answering) responses in an adapted alternating-treatments design. Picture (tact) prompts produced **fewer trials to criterion** than vocal (echoic) prompts for all three, though both eventually worked. — [PMC3177351 / DOI 10.1901/jaba.2011.44-659](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3177351)
- **Conflicting result: Ingvarsson & Le (2011).** Four autistic children met criterion faster with echoic prompts than with tact or textual prompts. Across the five direct comparisons that later literature lists, three favoured textual or tact prompts. Echoic prompts carry a risk of echoic responding under poor stimulus control: the child repeats any vocal stimulus. This comes from search-surfaced summaries, and the primary was not read. — [Springer DOI 10.1007/BF03393093 (as surfaced; verify)](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF03393093); [PMC3177351](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3177351)
- **Ingvarsson & Hollobaugh (2010, JABA).** Children were taught to say "I don't know, please tell me" to unknown questions. The response generalised to untrained unknown questions for three participants. It is a functional alternative to guessing or echoing. — [NCAEP resource record](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/resource/acquisition-of-intraverbal-behavior-teaching-children-with-autism-to-mand-for-answers-to-questions/)

**Echolalia: "echo the answer, not the question"**
- **Cues-pause-point (CPP).** The trainer cues silence, asks the question, pauses, then points to the stimulus. This teaches the learner to emit the label (answer) rather than echo the question.
  - **McMorrow & Foxx (1986).** With a 21-year-old autistic man, echolalia was quickly replaced by correct answers, with broader generalised gains.
  - **Dyer et al. (1987, JABA).** CPP taught echolalic individuals to use their labelling repertoires functionally.
  - **Foxx et al. (1988).** Correct answers replaced maladaptive speech in training and in all generalisation settings.
  - **Foxx & Faw (1990).** A 26–57-month follow-up of six people found echolalia below baseline in 80.6% of follow-ups (MODERATE, older single-case work, mostly adults or older children).

  The mapping of individual PMC IDs to specific papers was not verified one by one. — [PSU record: CPP teaching echolalics](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/cuespausepoint-language-training-teaching-echolalics-functional-u/); [PSU record: CPP structuring trainer statements](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/cuespausepoint-language-training-structuring-trainer-statements-t/); [PMC1285948](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1285948); [PMC1286251](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1286251)
- **A 2011 IMFAR abstract** also reported CPP reducing echolalia and increasing correct answers in a child with autism (WEAK). — [IMFAR 2011](https://imfar.confex.com/imfar/2011/webprogram/Paper8673.html)

**Time delay and prompt fading**
- **Walker (2008, JADD).** The review covered 22 studies of constant (CTD) and progressive (PTD) time delay with autistic children. They performed similarly on some measures, and none of the earlier studies compared them directly. A summary of the review reports that maximum delays commonly ranged from 5–10 s, usually increased 1–2 s at a time. — [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/e36d94ae-31c2-4902-817d-c7e6fc4527c8); [UNR NDSIP tip sheet](https://www.unr.edu/ndsip/services/resources/tips/progressive-time-delay-prompting)
- **A 2020 *Journal of Behavioral Education* comparison** found progressive prompt delay generally the most efficient method, including on error rates, because it leaves less time for errors early in training. Earlier comparisons were mixed: one found 5-s CTD marginally more efficient. — [Springer DOI 10.1007/s10864-020-09407-0](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10864-020-09407-0)
- **Taylor & Harris (1995, JABA 28(1):3–14).** Time delay taught three autistic children to ask "What's that?" when novel stimuli appeared. It generalised to a new room, a new person and new stimuli. — [PMC1279781](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1279781)
- **Script fading.** Krantz & McClannahan (1993, JABA 26(1):121–132) faded written scripts from end to beginning for four autistic children. Initiations generalised to a new setting, time, teacher and activity, three of four maintained them at two months, and the final levels fell within the range of non-disabled peers. — [PMC1297725](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1297725)
- **Script fading for non-readers.** A variant uses audio-recorded scripts. — [NCAEP resource record](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/resource/social-interaction-skills-for-children-with-autism-a-script%E2%80%90fading-procedure-for-nonreaders/)

**Errorless learning vs trial-and-error / error correction**
- **Leaf et al. (2020, *Analysis of Verbal Behavior* 36(1):1–20).** This RCT randomised 28 autistic children to error correction or errorless learning, delivered as most-to-least echoic prompting (full, then partial, then none).
  - Both worked: 97.6% vs 95.2% of sets were mastered.
  - Sessions to mastery did not differ (about 16; p = .643).
  - Post-test correct responding was higher for error correction (85.7% vs 77.1%, p = .039).
  - Aberrant behaviour was rare in both arms.
  - Maintenance was not measured. Participants had relatively strong verbal repertoires, and the authors are developers of flexible prompt fading.

  — [PMC7343685 / DOI 10.1007/s40616-019-00124-y](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7343685)
- **Flexible prompt fading.** Studies, largely from the Leaf group, show it is about as effective as constant time delay, with efficiency idiosyncratic to the learner. One study found higher independent responding than most-to-least prompting. Independent replication is limited. — [PMC5118260](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5118260); [Autism Partnership Foundation summary](https://autismpartnershipfoundation.org/?p=869)

**Receptive identification and picture-selection teaching (directly relevant to "tap the right photo")**
- **Grow & LeBlanc (2013, *Behavior Analysis in Practice* 6(1):56–75)** give recommendations for receptive-language programmes. On arrays and position:
  - **Avoid two-item arrays.** With two comparisons, a side bias is reinforced 50% of the time and errors are hard to detect. Use **at least three** comparisons ("a good rule of thumb").
  - Rotate the position of the correct comparison and the auditory instruction across trials in a balanced way (left, middle and right proportionally), and track balance on a data sheet.
  - Teach targets simultaneously, within conditional discriminations, rather than in isolation or in massed single-item blocks.

  On attention and prompting:
  - Require an observing response. Use a differential observing response for learners with faulty stimulus control.
  - Avoid extra-stimulus prompts for learners who show overselectivity.
  - Fade prompts systematically to prevent prompt dependence.

  On reinforcement and cueing:
  - Reserve the best reinforcers for independent correct responses, and use intermittent or no reinforcement for prompted ones.
  - Avoid inadvertent cues such as gaze, hand position or stressed words.
  - Use concise instructions, and keep the defining feature of the target out of the incorrect comparisons.

  (MODERATE: expert recommendations grounded in single-case literature. The authors note that array size has been little tested.) — [PMC3680153 / DOI 10.1007/BF03391791](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3680153)
- **Grow (CIRCA presentation slides)**, summarising this work, ties position rotation explicitly to preventing side bias. — [Grow CIRCA slides](https://sandbox-circa-educ.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/Laura-Grow-CIRCA-presentation-Oct-14_0.pdf)

**Mastery criteria**
- **McDougale, Richling, Longino & O'Rourke (2020, *Behavior Analysis in Practice* 13(2):402–410).**
  - In 157 skill-acquisition studies (2015–2017), the most common criterion was 90% accuracy, used in 32%, and two sessions was the most common requirement (60%).
  - Practitioners most often use 80% across three sessions.
  - Only 41% of studies probed maintenance.
  - Cited evidence suggests 80% across three sessions is often insufficient for maintenance, 90% across three sessions is inconsistent, and 100% across three sessions is most effective.
  - The authors recommend higher criteria plus scheduled maintenance probes.

  — [PMC7314871 / DOI 10.1007/s40617-019-00365-2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7314871)
- **Richling, Williams & Carr (2019, JABA 52(3):701–717).** An 80%-across-three-sessions criterion did not reliably produce maintenance. Pitts & Hoerger (2020) reported better maintenance with 100% criteria. Search summaries conflict on the exact criteria Richling compared (60/80/100% vs 80/90/100%). — [Search-surfaced PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9120273); [Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/555151242/RichlingWilliamsCarr2019)

### Inferences
- The common components are:
  1. a picture or visual cue that makes the answer category visible;
  2. a prompt that models only the answer;
  3. systematic fading, such as time delay;
  4. stronger reinforcement for independent than for prompted answers;
  5. many exemplars;
  6. deliberate mixing of WH-types and probing for generalisation.

  An app can automate items 1–4 and 6 well. Item 5 depends on the parent supplying enough varied photos.
- Secan et al. found generalisation came "for free" only when the relevant cue was visible. So photo-based "who" practice will likely transfer to *seen* situations (pointing at photos, at people in the room) before it transfers to questions about *absent* referents ("Who picked you up today?"). Those need separate, later programming.
- Raulston et al. and Barthold show that learning one WH-word does not reliably transfer to others. An app that only ever asks "who" cannot show the child understands *who* as opposed to responding to the verb or the picture. Mixed-WH discrimination is essential (see section 5).
- The echolalia literature (CPP) and the echoic-prompt risk suggest that any spoken prompt should be the answer alone ("Daddy"), never a re-statement of the question. The app should pause between question and prompt, which mirrors CPP.
- Mastery should be set higher than the habitual 80%, e.g., 90–100% independent across two to three sessions, and followed by maintenance probes.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that specifically isolates teaching *who* questions (person-identity answers) to young autistic children with photos of their own family. Who is usually bundled with what/where.
- The full texts of Secan et al. (1989) and Jahr (2001) were not accessible. Participant ages, the exact colour-cue design and the cue-fading steps could not be verified.
- No source found on whether children *should* first learn "who" with identity questions ("Who is this?") before action-based questions ("Who is eating?"). The developmental ordering of who versus what/where in autism was not found; Goodwin et al. tested *what*, not *who*.
- Fitzgerald key and the 1980s–90s Goldstein work were not located in this pass.
- No direct evidence was found on whether tapping a picture (a listener/selection response) transfers to *saying* the answer vocally.

## 2. How do naturalistic approaches (NDBIs: PRT, JASPER, ESDM, incidental teaching) teach question-answering, and how might an app support rather than replace them?

### Takeaway
NDBIs embed teaching in play and daily routines. They use shared control, child-chosen materials and natural reinforcers, and they have an established evidence base, including RCT-level evidence for some models. An app is best positioned as a short, structured practice and photo-generation tool that feeds the parent's natural "who" questions during real routines, not as a substitute.

### Cited Findings
- **Schreibman et al. (2015, JADD 45:2411–2428)** define NDBIs as interventions that:
  - are implemented in natural settings;
  - involve shared control between child and therapist;
  - use natural contingencies;
  - use a range of behavioural strategies.

  The definition was proposed to unify models with different names, including PRT, JASPER, ESDM and incidental teaching. — [PubMed 25737021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25737021/); [PMC4513196](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4513196)
- **Frost et al. (2020)** built an eight-item observational fidelity measure (NDBI-Fi) for caregivers' use of common NDBI elements across models, supporting parent-implemented NDBI. — [Frost et al. 2020 postprint](https://autismlab.psy.msu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Frost-et-al.-2020-NDBI-Fi-Postprint.pdf)
- **NCAEP 2020 report.** The review covers literature from 1990–2017 and identifies 28 EBPs and 10 manualised interventions. PRT, JASPER, Milieu Training, Project ImPACT and PECS are among the named practices with sufficient evidence. — [NCAEP 2020 report page](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/?p=395); [FPG executive summary page](https://fpg.unc.edu/publications/executive-summary-evidence-based-practices-children-youth-and-young-adults-autism)
- **Kasari et al. (2014, JAACAP 53(6):635–646).** This SMART trial randomised 61 minimally verbal autistic children aged 5–8 to JASP+EMT, with or without a speech-generating device (SGD). The combination with the SGD gave better spontaneous communicative utterances, novel words and comments. This shows technology can boost a naturalistic intervention when it is embedded in it (STRONG). — [PubMed 24839882](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24839882/); [PMC4030683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4030683)
- **Ganz et al. (2014).** In tablet vocabulary studies, generalisation appeared when parents or teachers, rather than researchers, delivered the prompts (as summarised in Urrea et al. 2024). — [PMC11137377](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11137377/)
- **Goodwin & Fein.** Mothers' use of WH-questions with verbs and complex WH-questions predicted later WH-comprehension in typically developing children, a caregiver-input effect. The same pattern was not reported for the autistic group in the snippet. — [Journal of Child Language abstract](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/abs/role-of-maternal-input-in-the-development-of-whquestion-comprehension-in-autism-and-typical-development/64AE4D72537C1C0CCF08ECFEEC5103BE)

### Inferences
- The app can support NDBI practice in several ways:
  - capturing photos of the child's real routines, such as Dad eating breakfast or Grandma reading;
  - giving the parent a daily "ask it in real life" prompt that uses the same people and actions;
  - letting the child choose which photo set or theme to play;
  - using naturally meaningful consequences where possible, e.g., a short video of that family member waving or saying the child's name.
- Kasari 2014 suggests technology works best as an *added* support inside responsive adult–child interaction. A parent-and-child co-play mode, with the parent sitting with the child and the app as a shared activity, is likely better than solo play.

### Gaps
- No study found that specifically teaches WH-question answering through PRT, JASPER or ESDM procedures. The NDBI literature targets broader language and social communication.
- Sandbank et al.'s (2020) NDBI meta-analysis was not retrieved in this pass. The pooled NDBI effect sizes are therefore not reported here.

## 3. Does tablet/app-based instruction work for autistic children, and does it generalise?

### Takeaway
Tablet and computer instruction is an NCAEP-recognised evidence-based practice (Technology-Aided Instruction and Intervention). Single-case meta-analyses show large within-study effects. However:
- group and RCT evidence is small to null, especially for broad language outcomes;
- head-to-head comparisons with flashcards or people find no consistent advantage for tablets;
- generalisation to real-world behaviour is the main weakness.

Personalised family photos have intuitive appeal, but I found no controlled evidence that they beat generic photos. Colour photographs beat cartoons for word retention.

### Cited Findings
**Evidence-based-practice status**
- **NCAEP / AFIRM.** Technology-Aided Instruction and Intervention (TAII) is instruction in which technology is the central feature supporting the learner's goal. It met NCAEP criteria with **17 single-case and 26 group-design studies**, and was effective from early intervention (0–2 years) and preschool (3–5) upward. — [AFIRM TAII brief packet (2025)](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Technology-Aided-Instruction-Intervention-Brief-Packet-Hedges-AFIRM-Team-Updated-2025.pdf); [AFIRM TAII module](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/node/1095)

**Meta-analyses (figures from abstracts or search snippets, so verify before quoting)**

Effect sizes from single-case nonoverlap indices (Tau, Tau-U) are not comparable to group effect sizes (d, g), and they tend to be high.

**Vocabulary and receptive identification (closest to the planned app)**
- **Urrea, Fernández-Torres, Rodriguez-Ortiz & Saldaña (2024, *Frontiers in Psychology* 15:1370965; PROSPERO CRD42021238758).** This systematic review covers 13 studies of tablet or computer vocabulary teaching for autistic children aged 0–16 (2006–2022).
  - Results: five studies were positive, six mixed, one negative and one null.
  - Pellecchia et al. (2020) was a large null trial (n = 154) of computer-assisted instruction on receptive and expressive language.
  - Head-to-head comparisons:
    - Chebli et al. (2019): 9 of 14 concepts generalised faster with an instructor, 5 faster with the tablet.
    - Pellegrino et al. (2020): all participants mastered faster with flashcards, though some preferred the tablet.
    - Allen et al. (2015): no advantage for an iPad over picture books (n = 16).
  - Effective app features included least-to-most prompting (Ganz 2014), a 10-s prompt delay (Kagohara 2012) and a preferred video embedded as the reinforcer (Chebli 2017).
  - The authors conclude that tablets "might be useful tools" for some children, but the evidence is too heterogeneous for firm conclusions.

  — [PMC11137377 / DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1370965](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11137377/)
- **Single-case report (2024, *Frontiers in Psychiatry*).** A six-year-old learned receptive labels faster with flashcards than with a tablet. Generalisation and maintenance did not differ at three weeks (WEAK). — [PMC11668748](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11668748/)
- **iPad vs flashcards, two participants.** iPad-assisted instruction gave slightly faster acquisition with fewer prompts (WEAK). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347683461_A_Comparison_of_iPad-Assisted_and_Flash_Card-Assisted_Instruction_for_Learners_With_Autism)

**Generalisation to real life**
- **Fletcher-Watson et al. (2016), "Click-East" RCT of the FindMe iPad app.** 54 autistic children under six took part. Children engaged consistently and parents rated the app highly. However, there were **no significant group differences** in parent-reported social communication or in observed parent–child play at follow-up (STRONG evidence that app engagement does not automatically produce real-world gains). — [PMC5015758](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5015758); [University of Edinburgh news](https://www.ed.ac.uk/clinical-brain-sciences/news/news-archive/jul-dec-2015/click-east-findings)

**Personalised photos vs generic images**
- **Carter & Hartley (2020/2021, JADD 51(9):3050–3062).** 20 autistic children retained novel word–picture pairs significantly better from **colour photographs than black-and-white cartoons** (1.88/4 vs 1.28/4).
  - The study did *not* test personal or family photos.
  - The authors caution that highly iconic images might reduce generalisation, an open question.

  — [PMC8349349 / DOI 10.1007/s10803-020-04771-2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349349/)
- **Iconicity in minimally verbal autistic children.** They made more errorless retrievals with the most iconic photographs. — [ERIC EJ1064778 (*Autism*, 2015)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1064778)
- **Extraneous detail.** A master's thesis found that materials without extraneous stimuli reduced trials to criterion for new receptive labels (WEAK). — [CSUMB thesis](https://digitalcommons.csumb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1505&context=caps_thes)
- **Unsupported claims.** Vendor blog claims that personalised visuals are "more effective" cite no study and should not be relied on. — [Vizyplan blog (cited as an example of weak evidence)](https://vizyplan.com/blog/personalized-visuals-neurodivergent-children-routines)
- **Personalised software.** Mulholland et al. (2008) programmed an animated tutor with each child's name and target words, but personalisation was not isolated as a variable (in Urrea et al. 2024). — [PMC11137377](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11137377/)

### Inferences
- Realistic expectations for parents:
  - A well-designed app is a reasonable supplement for building the *receptive* "who" discrimination.
  - It is not likely to be faster than a parent with printed photos.
  - It will not produce real-world question-answering unless real-life practice is built in.
  - The FindMe RCT is the clearest cautionary result.
- Family photos are a sensible choice on face validity: the targets are the actual people the child must identify, so stimuli are "real-world" by construction. The evidence-supported reasons are that they are colour photographs (Carter & Hartley) and the real targets. "Personalisation improves learning" is not established.
- To protect generalisation, use **multiple different photos of each person**: different clothes, angles, rooms and lighting, as in multiple-exemplar training. Highly uniform, highly iconic images risk the child learning the picture rather than the person.

### Gaps
- No controlled study compared personal or family photos with generic photos for autistic children.
- Author names and full numbers for the 2017 tablet single-case meta-analysis and the 2018 iPad meta-analysis could not be verified (paywall, 403).
- No study found on app-based teaching of WH-questions specifically for preschool autistic children.

## 4. What do HCI research, accessibility standards and privacy rules say about designing apps for autistic children?

### Takeaway
The HCI literature converges on several principles:
- visual simplicity and consistency;
- predictable navigation;
- redundant (image, sound and text) presentation;
- customisation of sensory features;
- clear, immediate feedback;
- large touch targets for young children.

Most of it is expert or consensus guidance from small-sample studies, not controlled trials (WEAK–MODERATE). Children's photos are personal data under COPPA, GDPR and India's DPDP regime. If the app ever leaves the family's own device, verifiable parental consent and data minimisation obligations apply.

### Cited Findings
**HCI and usability guidance**
- **Britto & Pizzolato ("GAIA"/AutismGuide).** Recommendations for autistic users fall into ten categories:
  - Customisation;
  - Visual and textual vocabulary;
  - Engagement;
  - System status;
  - Redundant representation (images, sound and text according to user preference; multiple formats);
  - Multimedia;
  - Affordance;
  - Navigability;
  - Interaction with touch screens;
  - Feedback.

  — [Britto & Pizzolato slides (via SlideShare mirror)](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/towards-web-accessibility-guidelines-of-interaction-and-interface-design-for-people-with-autism-spectrum-disorder/61599491); [SBC IHC comparative analysis](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/ihc/article/view/22274)
- **Aguiar et al. ("AutismGuide", *Behaviour & Information Technology* 2022, 41(6):1132–1150).** A systematic literature review produced 155 recommendations, consolidated into 69. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346812465_AutismGuide_a_usability_guidelines_to_design_software_solutions_for_users_with_autism_spectrum_disorder); [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:tbitxx:v:41:y:2022:i:6:p:1132-1150)
- **Systematic review of mobile app development for autistic children (2018–2025).** 15 of 78 records met the inclusion criteria. Many studies used small samples and short testing periods, which limits generalisability. — [JASCI (ThaiJO)](https://ph01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JASCI/article/view/265621)
- **Systematic mapping of accessible interfaces for autistic children.** It proposes a design-guidance catalogue, including soft colours and readable typography, applied to a mobile app. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/412944130_A_Systematic_Mapping_of_the_Literature_on_the_Development_of_Accessible_Digital_Interfaces_for_Children_with_Autism_Spectrum_Disorder_ASD)
- **Usability-guideline review for mobile UIs for autistic children (2018).** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327924872_A_review_on_usability_guidelines_for_designing_mobile_apps_user_interface_for_children_with_autism)
- **Critical HCI review.** Most autism technology purposes (behaviour analysis, assistive technology, education, social skills, therapy, well-being) are defined from outside. Few designs build on children's own interests or agency. — [ResearchGate: Designing Interactive Technology to Support Children with Autism](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380527559_Designing_Interactive_Technology_to_Support_Children_with_Autism)
- **W3C COGA, "Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities"** (W3C Working Group Note, 2021). It is supplemental to WCAG. Its patterns include:
  - make the purpose clear;
  - use a design the user is likely to recognise;
  - use a consistent visual design;
  - make each step clear;
  - avoid processes that depend on memory.

  — [W3C TR coga-usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable); [Design Guide section](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/design_guide.html)

**Young children and touch**
- **Nielsen Norman Group** recommends touch targets of at least **2 cm × 2 cm** for young children, about four times the 1 cm × 1 cm it cites for adults. This is practitioner guidance. — [NN/g: Children's UX – physical development](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/)
- **Woodward/Anthony et al. (CHI 2016).** Children have more difficulty with touch interactions than adults, partly due to target size and location. — [Woodward et al. CHI 2016 PDF](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/378/2017/05/Woodward-et-al-CHI2016_0.pdf)
- **Vatavu, Cramariuc & Schipor (2015, *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*)** studied tap, double-tap and drag-and-drop with 3–6-year-olds on tablets and phones. The specific size numbers were not retrieved. — [Springer chapter record](https://rd.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-22668-2_42)

**Reinforcement and feedback inside apps (behavioural evidence)**
- **Grow & LeBlanc (2013)** recommend continuous reinforcement for independent correct responses and the best-quality reinforcers reserved for them. Prompted responses get intermittent or no reinforcement. Regular preference assessments should identify several reinforcers. — [PMC3680153](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3680153)
- **Chebli et al. (2017)** embedded a preferred video in the app as the reinforcer (in Urrea et al. 2024). — [PMC11137377](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11137377/)

**Privacy and children's data**
- **US COPPA amended rule.**
  - Published in the Federal Register on 22 April 2025 and effective 23 June 2025; most obligations had to be met by **22 April 2026**.
  - "Personal information" now covers biometric identifiers usable for automated or semi-automated recognition. The FTC says this includes photographs converted into recognition templates.
  - Operators must publish a data-retention policy for children's personal information.

  — [Federal Register 2025-05904 (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm); [Federal Register page](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule); [Hunton summary](https://hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/ftc-publishes-final-coppa-rule-amendments)
- **GDPR Article 8.** Where consent is the basis for information society services offered directly to a child under 16, consent must be given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility. Member States may lower the age to no less than 13; the UK uses 13. — [legislation.gov.uk Art. 8](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/8); [ICO guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/guide-to-the-general-data-protection-regulation-gdpr/children-and-the-gdpr/what-are-the-rules-about-an-iss-and-consent/); [EU FRA mapping](https://fra.europa.eu/en/publication/2017/mapping-minimum-age-requirements-concerning-rights-child-eu/consent-use-data-children)
- **India DPDP Rules 2025 (notified around 13–14 November 2025).**
  - Rule 10 requires Data Fiduciaries to adopt technical and organisational measures that obtain **verifiable parental consent** before processing a child's personal data.
  - They must also check, with due diligence, that the consenting parent is an identifiable adult (18 or over).
  - Verification draws on identity and age details already held, details voluntarily provided, or a virtual token from an authorised entity such as DigiLocker.

  — [DPDP Rule 10 text](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule10.html); [LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/amp/top-stories/digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025-notified-309928)
- **DPDP Act section 9 and exemptions.** It requires verifiable consent from a parent or lawful guardian for children and persons with disabilities, and bars processing likely to harm a child. Commentators note a restriction on tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted advertising directed at children. Narrow exemptions exist, e.g., for healthcare and educational institutions, which apply only where no tracking or behavioural monitoring occurs. — [DPDP Rule 10 page (s.9 summary)](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule10.html); [Bar & Bench](https://www.barandbench.com/news/centre-notifies-digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025-how-different-are-they-from-the-draft-rules)
- **DPDP scope and commencement.** Processing for purely **personal or domestic purposes** is excluded from the Act. The core obligations (ss. 3–17) take effect 18 months after notification, around **13 May 2027**; sources differ by a day. — [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/indias-digital-personal-data-protection-act-2023-brought-into-force-); [Mondaq phased rollout](https://webiis10.mondaq.com/india/data-protection/1706878/indias-digital-personal-data-protection-act-phased-rollout-and-key-compliance-milestones); [TaxGuru](https://taxguru.in/corporate-law/dpdp-act-2023-simple-guide-businesses-india.html)

### Inferences
- Privacy, depending on how the app is distributed:
  - **Family-only, on-device:** a parent building the app solely for their own child, with photos stored only on the family device, is likely within the DPDP personal or domestic exclusion. This should be confirmed with counsel.
  - **Published or cloud-synced:** if the app is ever published, used by other families or synced to a cloud backend, COPPA, GDPR-K and DPDP obligations bite. These include verifiable parental consent, minimisation, a retention policy and no behavioural tracking or ads. Family photos of a child are personal data; face templates are biometric under the amended COPPA rule.
  - **Practical defaults:** keep data local, use no analytics SDKs, add no face recognition, and provide a simple "delete all photos and data" control.
- The HCI guidance is consistent with the behavioural evidence. Both favour a sparse screen, consistent layout, distinctive but non-distracting feedback and customisable sound. That makes these low-risk defaults even though they rest on weak evidence.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled HCI study showing that error sounds or "buzzers" harm autistic children's learning. The advice to avoid negative sounds rests on sensory-sensitivity rationale and the errorless-learning tradition, not direct trials.
- Exact DPDP section 9 statutory wording, the UK Age Appropriate Design Code and GDPR's household exemption (Art. 2(2)(c)) were not retrieved in this pass.
- No evidence-based numbers were found for ideal session length or animation limits for autistic preschoolers.

## 5. Concrete, evidence-linked design implications for a "who"-question app

### Takeaway
The parent's idea (family photos, a spoken "Who is …?", picture choices and a celebration) matches the evidence-based core of picture-prompted question teaching. Four changes would make it much more likely to work and to generalise:
- avoid a fixed two-choice array, or counterbalance it rigorously and move quickly to three choices;
- build errorless-to-independent prompt fading (time delay) into the logic;
- add a mixed-WH discrimination phase, so the child actually learns what *who* means and is not just matching the verb;
- tie mastery rules, maintenance checks and real-life practice prompts to a parent dashboard.

### Cited Findings
These are the evidence anchors for the implications below. All are repeated from sections 1–4.
- Two-item arrays reinforce side bias 50% of the time. Use three or more comparisons, rotate positions in a balanced way and teach targets simultaneously. — [Grow & LeBlanc 2013, PMC3680153](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3680153)
- Give the best reinforcers for independent correct responses and less or none for prompted ones. Avoid inadvertent cues and keep instructions concise. — [Grow & LeBlanc 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3680153)
- Picture prompts reached criterion in fewer trials than echoic prompts in one study, while another favoured echoic prompts. Echoic prompts risk echolalic responding. — [Ingvarsson & Hollobaugh 2011, PMC3177351](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3177351)
- Cues-pause-point replaces echolalia with correct answers. — [PSU CPP record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/cuespausepoint-language-training-teaching-echolalics-functional-u/)
- Progressive time delay is efficient with low error rates. Delays typically step up by 1–2 s to a maximum of 5–10 s. — [JBE 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10864-020-09407-0); [UNR NDSIP](https://www.unr.edu/ndsip/services/resources/tips/progressive-time-delay-prompting)
- Errorless and error-correction approaches were both effective, with similar efficiency. — [Leaf et al. 2020, PMC7343685](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7343685)
- Generalisation across WH-types is not automatic. Multiple-exemplar training produced novel-question answering. Visible-cue questions generalise, while absent-referent questions need extra programming. — [Raulston 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1750946713000597); [Jahr 2001](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222874784_Teaching_children_with_autism_to_answer_novel_wh-questions_by_utilizing_a_multiple_exemplar_strategy); [Secan 1989](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1286168/)
- 80% across three sessions is often insufficient for maintenance, and 90–100% plus maintenance probes is better. — [McDougale et al. 2020, PMC7314871](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7314871)
- An engaging app did not change real-world social communication in an RCT. Technology added *within* adult-led naturalistic intervention improved spontaneous language. — [FindMe RCT, PMC5015758](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5015758); [Kasari 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24839882/)
- Colour photographs beat cartoons for retention, with no evidence either way on personal photos. — [Carter & Hartley 2020, PMC8349349](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349349/)
- Touch targets for young children should be at least 2 × 2 cm. Use a consistent, simple, predictable design. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/); [W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable)
- Children's photos are personal data, verifiable parental consent applies, and amended COPPA covers biometrics. — [COPPA FR 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm); [DPDP Rule 10](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule10.html); [GDPR Art. 8](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/8)

### Inferences
These are design implications reasoned from the evidence above; each names its anchor.

**A. Teaching logic**
1. **Phase 0, a preference and photo check.** The parent picks two to four highly familiar people and several photos of each, from different days, clothes and settings, to build multiple exemplars. The app also offers a reinforcer menu, such as a favourite short clip, sound or animation, chosen by the parent or child. (Anchor: Jahr, Grow & LeBlanc preference assessment)
2. **Phase 1, "Who is this?" as person identification.** Strictly, with picture choices this is a listener task: "Where's Daddy?" or "Touch Daddy". A true "Who is this?" shows *one* photo and asks for the *name*. There are two options:
   - Offer name choices: the parent's recorded voice says "Daddy" and "Mummy", shown as buttons with small faces, which is closer to a tact or intraverbal.
   - Teach the listener version first ("Touch Daddy"), then the speaker version (a single photo plus "Who is this?", with the child saying or selecting the name). The speaker version needs a voice-recording or parent-scored mode.

   (Anchor: Grow & LeBlanc conditional discrimination; the gap on listener-to-speaker transfer)
3. **Phase 2, "Who is eating?" as action-based who.** Show two to three people. Only one is doing the named action, and the others do different actions. Use several actions (eating, sleeping, hugging, driving). (Anchor: multiple-exemplar training)
4. **Phase 3, the mixed-WH discrimination phase. This is critical.** Mix "Who is eating?" with "What is Daddy eating?" on the *same* scene. The choice array then contains both people and objects, such as Daddy, a banana and a car, so the child must use the WH-word to choose a category.
   - Without this phase, "Who is eating?" can be answered by attending only to "eating".
   - Optionally add "Where is Daddy?" (kitchen or park) later.
   - Optional colour-coded WH cues can be shown and faded, e.g., a "who" icon of a face silhouette and a "what" icon of an object silhouette.

   (Anchor: Secan 1989 visual cues; Raulston 2013 no cross-WH transfer; Jahr 2001)
5. **Array size and position.**
   - Prefer **three choices** from the start. If two are used for the very first sessions, counterbalance left and right exactly, e.g., semi-random with no more than two consecutive same-side positions.
   - Log selections by position and flag side bias automatically, e.g., more than 70% same-side taps.
   - Move to three as soon as two-choice mastery is reached.
   - Always randomise which photo is correct and where it appears. Never fix the correct position.

   (Anchor: Grow & LeBlanc)
6. **No inadvertent cues.** All choices should be the same size, frame, brightness and crop, with no pre-response animation or glow on the correct item and no stressed words in the audio. Use concise audio ("Who is eating?"), with the question read the same way each time. (Anchor: Grow & LeBlanc)
7. **Errorless start, then progressive time delay.**
   - First trials for a new target run at 0-s delay. Immediately after the question, the correct photo is prompted, e.g., it gently enlarges or pulses, or the parent's voice says only the answer: "Daddy".
   - Over successive sessions or correct runs, the delay steps up by about 1 s, up to about 4–5 s, so the child can answer before the prompt.
   - Fade the prompt itself too, e.g., big pulse, then small pulse, then none.
   - As an alternative, start with least-to-most prompting for children who already respond well.

   For overselective children, prefer within-stimulus prompts, such as enlarging the correct photo, over extra-stimulus arrows. (Anchor: Walker 2008, the JBE 2020 comparison, Grow & LeBlanc, Leaf 2020)
8. **Echolalia-safe prompts ("echo the answer, not the question").** Any spoken prompt says only the answer label ("Daddy"), never "Who is eating? Daddy is eating". Insert a short pause between the question and any prompt, mirroring cues-pause-point. If the app ever accepts vocal answers, it should not count the child echoing "who is eating" as correct. (Anchor: CPP; Ingvarsson echoic-prompt risk)
9. **Differential reinforcement.**
   - An independent correct tap earns the big celebration: the chosen reinforcer, about 3–5 s, consistent and not over-stimulating.
   - A prompted correct tap earns a brief neutral "Yes, Daddy" with no big celebration.
   - An error gets no buzzer and no red X. The app calmly re-presents the trial with a 0-s prompt, as an errorless correction, then moves on.

   Thin the schedule later to intermittent celebration once performance is stable. (Anchor: Grow & LeBlanc; Leaf 2020 error correction vs errorless)
10. **Mastery and advancement rules.**
    - A target or phase is mastered at **90% or more independent correct across two consecutive sessions**, or 100% across one session of at least 10 trials. Make this a parent-adjustable setting.
    - The app then advances: it adds a person, adds an action, raises the array to three, then moves to the mixed-WH phase.
    - It automatically schedules **maintenance probes** of mastered items, e.g., at one, two and four weeks, interspersed among new trials.
    - If two sessions in a row fall below about 60%, it steps back one phase or reduces the delay.

    (Anchor: McDougale et al. 2020; Richling 2019)
11. **Interspersal.** Mix about 30% mastered or easy trials with new ones to keep success rates high and motivation up. This is consistent with high-success errorless approaches. (Inference: interspersal research was not retrieved in this pass.)
12. **Generalisation probes inside the app.** Periodically use never-before-seen photos of the same people and new actions without prompts or reinforcement, and report them separately to the parent. (Anchor: Secan, Jahr, Barthold)

**B. Interface and sensory design**
13. Show one task per screen, on a plain background. Use large, equally spaced photo targets of at least about 2 × 2 cm, which in practice is much larger for three photos on a tablet. Hide navigation from the child (no menus), and keep layout and button positions consistent across sessions. (Anchor: NN/g, W3C COGA, AutismGuide)
14. **Sound.** Provide a parent-adjustable volume and a choice of reinforcer sounds, or none. Avoid sudden loud sounds. Record the question in the parent's own voice, which also aids generalisation to the parent asking. Offer an on-screen text caption option for redundancy. (Anchor: AutismGuide categories on customisation and redundant representation)
15. **Sessions.** Keep them short and predictable, e.g., 10 trials, with a visible progress strip such as five stars filling and a clear "all done" screen. The parent sets the session length. (Inference: no direct evidence on optimal length was found.)
16. **Lock-in.** Use guided-access or kiosk mode so the child cannot exit or reach settings, with a parent gate to reach settings and data.

**C. Parent mode and real-life bridge**
17. **Parent dashboard.** Show accuracy (independent vs prompted) per target and phase, a side-bias flag, current delay level, mastery dates, maintenance results and generalisation-probe results. Data are kept locally. (Anchor: McDougale on documenting criteria and maintenance)
18. **"Ask it today" prompts.** After a session, show the parent one or two real-life practice cards, such as "At dinner, ask 'Who is eating?' Wait 3 seconds. If needed, point to Dad and say 'Daddy'". Encourage co-play, with the parent sitting with the child. (Anchor: FindMe RCT, Kasari 2014, Ganz 2014, NDBI principles)
19. **Photo capture from real routines.** Let parents take new photos directly into the app ("Grandma reading"), so the stimuli keep growing and match the child's real day. (Anchor: multiple-exemplar training and natural contexts)
20. **"I don't know" option (later stage).** For questions about unseen events, the parent can enable an "I don't know, tell me" button. (Anchor: Ingvarsson & Hollobaugh 2010)

**D. Privacy**
21. Store all photos and data on the device only, with no account, cloud, analytics, ads or face recognition. Provide a single "delete everything" control. If the app is ever shared with other families, add a verifiable parental consent flow and a privacy notice with a retention policy. That brings it into line with DPDP Rule 10 (from about May 2027 core obligations), GDPR Art. 8 and the amended COPPA rule. (Anchor: COPPA 2025, DPDP, GDPR Art. 8)

### Gaps
- None of these specific parameters has been tested in a controlled study of a "who" app: a 90% criterion, 1-s delay steps, 30% interspersal, a 70% side-bias flag or 10-trial sessions. They are reasoned defaults from the general procedures literature and should be adjustable.
- Whether a tap or selection version of "who" transfers to spoken answers for this child is unknown and should be monitored. A parent-scored "say it" mode lets the parent check this directly.
- No evidence was found on whether learning with the family's own photos generalises to *other* people, such as teachers or therapists. Including a few photos of non-family regular people would test this.
