# The Developmental Paediatrician / Clinical Perspective on Teaching WH-Questions ("Who") to Young Autistic Children, and on Using Apps and Screens to Do It

Scope note for the report writer: this file covers developmental norms, professional-body screen guidance, the AAP autism clinical report and evidence-based-practice reviews, clinician concerns, and practical clinical advice. It does NOT cover step-by-step behavioural teaching procedures for WH-questions or app UI design (covered by another researcher). Labels used below: **[GUIDELINE]** = official position of a professional or government body; **[RESEARCH]** = peer-reviewed study or review finding; **[CLINICIAN OPINION]** = individual clinician's view or parent-facing advice article; **[SECONDARY]** = news/blog coverage, lower confidence. Research date: 26 Nov 2026.

## 1. Developmental norms for WH-questions, prerequisites for "who", and why WH-questions are harder for autistic children

### Takeaway
Typically developing children begin responding to "where" questions in the second year, ask who/what/where/why questions by about age 3, and answer simple who/what/where questions around 3-4 years; "when", "why" and "how" come later. Autistic children understand WH-questions later than typically developing peers but at a similar *language level*, and their difficulty seems to involve both grammar and social-pragmatic understanding. The popular "gestalt language processing" explanation of echolalia is contested in the research literature.

### Cited Findings
**Typical-development milestones (official / professional-body milestone lists)**
- [GUIDELINE] CDC "Learn the Signs. Act Early." milestones (revised 2022; page last reviewed 16 May 2026) describe what **at least 75%** of children can do by an age. By **3 years**: "Asks 'who,' 'what,' 'where,' or 'why' questions, like 'Where is mommy/daddy?'"; "Says what action is happening in a picture or book when asked, like 'running,' 'eating,' or 'playing'"; "Says first name, when asked"; "Talks with you in conversation using at least two back-and-forth exchanges." — [CDC 3-year milestones](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)
- [GUIDELINE] CDC by **4 years**: "Answers simple questions like 'What is a coat for?' or 'What is a crayon for?'". The page also has a parent tip to "Take time to answer your child's 'why' questions." No separate who/why answering milestone is listed. — [CDC 4-year milestones](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html)
- [GUIDELINE] ASHA communication milestones, **2-3 years** (seen in an ASHA-hosted mirror; the main asha.org page returned 403 to my fetch): "Asks why and how. Answers questions like 'What do you do when you are sleepy?' or 'Which one can you wear?'" — [ASHA 2-3 years](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-2-to-3-years/) (text verified via [prep.asha.org mirror](https://prep.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-2-to-3-years))
- [GUIDELINE, via a reproduction] An ASHA-based chart lists at **13-18 months**: "Looks around when asked 'where' questions, like 'Where's your blanket?'" — [Reading Rockets reproduction of ASHA chart](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/speech-language-and-hearing/articles/what-expect-your-childs-communication-development-birth)
- [SECONDARY / university clinic handout following ASHA] "Answers simple who, what, and where questions" placed at **3-4 years**. — [Fort Hays State University milestones handout](https://www.fhsu.edu/commdis/herndon-clinic/milestones-handout-b-5.pdf)

**WH-questions in autism (research)**
- [RESEARCH] Goodwin, Fein & Naigles (2012), *Autism Research*: longitudinal study, 15 autistic children vs. 18 language-matched TD toddlers, home visits every 4 months over 3 years. Children with ASD "rarely produce wh-questions... in naturalistic speech." Comprehension of subject- and object-wh-questions was **delayed** in ASD compared with TD, and the authors asked whether this reflects social-pragmatic and/or grammatical difficulty. In TD and ASD alike, comprehension came before production. — [Goodwin et al. 2012, PMC3329597](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3329597)
- [RESEARCH] Search-result summaries of the same literature report that autistic children understood WH-questions *at a later age* but *at a similar level of overall language development* as TD children. They also report the hypothesis that autistic children may ask fewer WH-questions because of "impaired understanding that others can have knowledge that would inform the purpose of their questions" (a theory-of-mind / social-pragmatic account). — [Goodwin et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3329597); [Jyotishi et al. 2017, PMC5344911](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5344911)
- [RESEARCH] Jyotishi, Fein & Naigles (2017), *Frontiers in Psychology*: 14 ASD vs. 17 language-matched TD children. It is "controversial the degree to which such deficits derive from social-pragmatic requirements and/or because these are complex grammatical structures." The findings implicated **both grammar and social-pragmatics**: early SVO word-order knowledge predicted later WH-question comprehension in the ASD group. — [Jyotishi et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5344911)
- [RESEARCH] Goodwin, Fein & Naigles (2015), *Journal of Child Language*: mothers' use of WH-questions with verbs, and of complex WH-questions, predicted WH-comprehension in TD children. In the ASD group, mothers' WH-questions with "be" as the main verb (e.g., "What's this?", "Who is that?") **negatively** predicted comprehension. "TD children and children with ASD appear to use their linguistic input differently." — [Goodwin et al. 2015, PubMed 24461930](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24461930/)
- [RESEARCH] Jyotishi, Fein & Naigles (2017), *Research in Developmental Disabilities*: parent report on the Vineland of the child's production of "who/why" questions was "highly accurate" when checked against observed speech. Parent report is therefore a reasonable way to track this skill. — [Jyotishi et al. 2017 RIDD, PMC5266545](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5266545)
- [GUIDELINE] The AAP 2020 clinical report says communicative spoken phrases before age 4 are "a good prognostic sign" but phrase speech "may occur to at least age 10 years, especially in children with preserved nonverbal skills and evidence of social engagement." — [Hyman, Levy, Myers 2020, AAP clinical report (full-text PDF hosted by UW)](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)

**Echolalia / gestalt language processing (GLP)**
- [RESEARCH, critical review] Hutchins, Knox & Fletcher (2024), *Autism & Developmental Language Impairments*: the term "gestalt language processor" is "conceptually troubled", and "evidence is lacking that GLP represents a legitimate clinical entity". The authors also critique the Natural Language Acquisition (NLA) stage model of moving from delayed echolalia to spontaneous speech. They acknowledge that NLA "promotes several practices that while not unique to NLA are time-honored and evidence-based." — [Hutchins et al. 2024, PMC11113044](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11113044)
- [RESEARCH] A 2024 *Current Psychiatry Reports* linguistics review argues that GLP rests on a premise "without theoretical foundation". — [Springer s40474-024-00309-8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40474-024-00309-8)
- [RESEARCH, commentary] A 2025 ASHA *Perspectives* commentary questions the theoretical basis of GLP/NLA but says the clinical practices it promotes "can (and should) be used in interventions for autistic children, regardless of views about Gestalt Language Processing." — [ASHA Perspectives 2025](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/2025_PERSP-25-00055?download=true)

### Inferences
- Prerequisites for answering "Who is this?" with a 2-choice picture tap probably include: attending to a photo when a person points or asks (joint attention), recognising familiar people in photos, receptively matching a spoken name to a person, and understanding that "who" asks for a *person*. "Who is eating?" also requires understanding the action word ("eating"); CDC places labelling the action in a picture at about 3 years. These prerequisites are inferred from the milestone sequence above, not stated by a guideline.
- Goodwin 2015 found that "be"-copula WH-questions ("Who is this?") negatively predicted WH-comprehension in ASD. This is one correlational study and does not show that "Who is this?" prompts are harmful. It does fit the general clinical view that varied, verb-based questions in real interactions ("Who is eating?", "Who is drinking?") may support WH comprehension better than repeated labelling questions alone.
- Given the Hutchins et al. critique, the report should not present GLP as established science. Echolalia is a common feature of autistic language, but the explanation offered for it is still debated.

### Gaps
- I found no authoritative, standardised age norm for *comprehending/answering* "who" specifically. Commonly repeated figures (e.g., a small longitudinal study giving "what/where ~26 months, who ~28 months, how ~33 months, why ~35 months" for *production*, and Ervin-Tripp 1970's comprehension order "what, where, whose, whom, who, how, when") appeared only in search snippets, and I could not verify their primary sources. Treat them as unverified.
- The ASHA Practice Portal pages (autism; language norms) returned 403, so their wording could not be quoted directly.
- No source directly measured joint attention alongside WH-question performance in autistic children; the joint-attention link is inferred.

## 2. What paediatric bodies say about screen time and apps for young children (AAP, IAP, WHO, CPS, RCPCH/UK), and specifically autistic children

### Takeaway
Every major body says: **no screens under ~18-24 months except video-chatting** (IAP allows only "minimal and occasional" video calls with distant family). For **2-5 years**, they say **about 1 hour/day or less** of high-quality, educational, preferably interactive content, ideally **shared with a caregiver**, with screen-free meals and bedtimes. The AAP's January 2026 policy statement moves away from counting minutes alone and focuses on content quality, context, co-use and "engagement-based design." No body has a separate screen-time guideline for autistic children. The CPS notes that behavioural effects are "more pronounced in children with special neurodevelopmental needs," and AAP parent advice for autism stresses shared use and avoiding auto-advance and reward hooks.

### Cited Findings
**American Academy of Pediatrics (USA)**
- [GUIDELINE] AAP "Media and Young Minds" policy statement, Council on Communications and Media, *Pediatrics* 2016;138(5):e20162591 (released Oct 2016; lead author Dimitri Christakis, with Jenny Radesky). - [GUIDELINE] **AAP 2026 update**: policy statement "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents" (AAP Council on Communications and Media; lead author Tiffany Munzer, MD, a developmental-behavioural paediatrician; *Pediatrics*, Feb 2026; published online 20 Jan 2026; DOI 10.1542/peds.2025-075320), with a companion technical report. It uses a socioecological model and age groups 0-5, 6-12 and 13-18. — [Contemporary Pediatrics, 29 Jan 2026](https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/aap-calls-for-system-level-changes-to-improve-children-s-digital-media-environments); [Technical report, PubMed 41556931](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41556931/); [MaineHealth repository listing](https://knowledgeconnection.mainehealth.org/mmc/4295)
- [GUIDELINE, as summarised by Contemporary Pediatrics] The 2026 statement says infants under 18 months have difficulty transferring information from screens to the real world. For preschoolers, high-quality educational content, "especially with caregiver involvement", is associated with better language and prosocial skills. Heavier noneducational and **solo** media use is linked to delays in language, cognition and social-emotional development, plus sleep disruption. "Joint media engagement, such as co-viewing and discussing content, is associated with improved learning and relational health." Children's media use "cannot be viewed solely through the lens of individual child behaviors or screen limits alone." — [Contemporary Pediatrics](https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/aap-calls-for-system-level-changes-to-improve-children-s-digital-media-environments)
- [GUIDELINE, as summarised] The 2026 statement contrasts **engagement-based design** ("endless scroll, autoplay, algorithmic recommender systems," and behavioural reinforcers that may prolong use and undermine child agency) with **child-centred design** (privacy, safety, developmental appropriateness, meaningful engagement). — [Contemporary Pediatrics](https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/aap-calls-for-system-level-changes-to-improve-children-s-digital-media-environments)
- [GUIDELINE, AAP parent-facing explainer] "Helping Kids Thrive in a Digital World: AAP Policy Explained" by Tiffany Munzer, MD, FAAP (HealthyChildren.org, last updated 3 Jun 2026). Families should "move beyond simple screen-time limits and focus on quality, context and conversation." "Infants under 18 months learn best from real-world interactions." A shared family tablet "can encourage co-watching", and personal tablets should be delayed. It recommends a Family Media Plan and screen-free mealtimes, bedrooms and the hour before bed. It names autoplay, endless scrolling, targeted ads, and apps "rewarding kids just for signing in" as business-driven features. The page gives **no** specific hour limits for 0-5. — [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/helping-kids-thrive-in-a-digital-world-AAP-policy-explained.aspx)
- [SECONDARY, conflicting] Parenting blogs disagree on whether the 2016 "1 hour/day for 2-5" cap still stands after the 2026 update. Some say minute-counting was dropped; others say the cap remains. — [Jellie's App blog](https://jelliesapp.com/blog/aap-screen-time-guidelines/); [Bloom Path blog](https://bloom-path.app/en/blog/aap-2026-screen-time-new-rules-en/). (Low-quality sources. The AAP's own explainer above emphasises quality over limits and gives no number.)
- [CLINICIAN OPINION, quoted in AAP coverage] Munzer: "When digital media are designed with children's wellbeing as the north star, young people can experience benefits, such as learning and social connection." — [AAP news release, 2026](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2026/american-academy-of-pediatrics-calls-for-creating-a-child-friendly-digital-world/) (quote surfaced via coverage; I did not fetch the release text directly)
- [GUIDELINE] **AAP "5 Cs of Media Use"** (AAP Center of Excellence on Social Media and Youth Mental Health): Child, Content, Calm, Crowding out, Communication. — [AAP 5 Cs Resource Hub](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/5cs-resource-hub/)
- [GUIDELINE] 5 Cs for toddlers/preschoolers (HealthyChildren.org, updated 30 Apr 2024): "try not to distract them with a device any time they're upset or bored"; "AVOID letting your child have open-access to a tablet or phone"; set aside daily time to play, read or go outside together; turn off autoplay; "If your child is watching something interesting, talk about it." — [HealthyChildren 5 Cs for toddlers and preschoolers](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/kids-and-screen-time-5-cs-questions-for-toddlers-and-preschoolers.aspx)

**AAP advice specific to autistic children (clinician article on AAP site)**
- [CLINICIAN OPINION, AAP-published] "How should I handle my child's media use? (autism spectrum disorder)", Jenny Radesky, MD, FAAP (developmental-behavioural paediatrician; Co-Medical Director, AAP Center of Excellence), updated 29 Mar 2023. It advises making media "a shared experience, rather than something kids do alone" and taking turns playing a favourite app together. - [GUIDELINE] "Indian Academy of Pediatrics Guidelines on Screen Time and Digital Wellness in Infants, Children and Adolescents" (Gupta P, Shah D, Bedi N, Galagali P, Dalwai S, et al.; IAP Guideline Committee), *Indian Pediatrics* 2022;59:235-244 (15 Mar 2022). - [GUIDELINE] IAP **24-59 months**: "Limit screen time to a maximum of 1 hour per day (with each session not more than 20-30 min); the lesser, the better." "Adult interaction with the child during media use was stressed upon." — [IAP 2022](https://www.indianpediatrics.net/mar2022/235.pdf)
- [GUIDELINE, evidence summary within IAP] "For children above 2 years of age, shared use of media between children and parents may help enhance learning interactions." ### Inferences
- The guidelines converge on the same points: (a) under 2, avoid screens other than video-calls with family; (b) 2-5, roughly ≤1 hour/day in short sessions (IAP: 20-30 min; Indian Psychiatric Society: ≤30 min × 2), educational and interactive; (c) **co-use** with an adult is the main quality factor; (d) no screens at meals, in the hour before bed, or to calm distress. An app that a parent always uses *with* the child, in short sessions, with educational intent, fits the letter and spirit of all these guidelines for a child aged 2 or over. Solo or unlimited use would not.
- The AAP 2026 framing (quality, context, design) supports judging a home-made app by its *design*: no autoplay, no endless loops, no engagement-maximising rewards, a natural stopping point. It does not support judging only by minutes.
- Because of the IAP's family-device and shared-use rules, an Indian clinician would likely advise running the app on a parent's phone or a shared tablet, not on a device the child owns.

### Gaps
- No AAP, IAP, WHO, CPS or RCPCH document gives **autism-specific** screen-time limits. Autism-specific advice exists only as clinician articles (Radesky) and the CPS remark about neurodevelopmental needs.
- I could not fetch the full text of the AAP 2026 policy statement (Pediatrics returned 403). Whether it formally retains the 2016 numeric limits for 2-5 years is unconfirmed; secondary sources conflict.
- I found no IAP update after the 2022 guideline. I found no WHO under-5 update after 2019.
- The exact wording of the UK Best Start in Life under-5 advice (Mar 2026) was not retrieved; only secondary summaries were.

## 3. Evidence-based interventions for young autistic children per the AAP 2020 clinical report and NCAEP 2020 — and where technology fits

### Takeaway
The AAP's 2020 clinical report (Hyman, Levy, Myers) supports early, individualised intervention based on ABA principles, including comprehensive models (EIBI, ESDM) and **naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions (NDBIs)**, and highlights **parent-mediated intervention** as having the most RCTs of any non-drug approach. It says **AAC does not delay speech** and may help it. It treats technology as a *delivery vehicle* inside a therapeutic plan, not a stand-alone treatment. NCAEP (2020) lists "Technology-Aided Instruction & Intervention" (TAII) as an evidence-based practice for ages 0-5. That evidence covers technology used for systematic instruction in planned interventions, not commercial apps in general.

### Cited Findings
**AAP 2020 clinical report: "Identification, Evaluation, and Management of Children With Autism Spectrum Disorder"** (Hyman SL, Levy SE, Myers SM; Council on Children with Disabilities, Section on Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrics; *Pediatrics* 2020;145(1):e20193447; DOI 10.1542/peds.2019-3447). It updates and replaces the two 2007 AAP autism reports.
- [GUIDELINE] It recommends standardised ASD screening at 18 and 24 months plus ongoing developmental surveillance. ASD "can be diagnosed as young as 18 months of age, and has evidenced-based interventions that may improve function." "Shared decision making calls for collaboration with families in evaluation and choice of interventions." — [PubMed 31843864 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31843864/); [Full text PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] Comprehensive treatment models (CTMs) "should be replicable, intense, and designed to address multiple therapeutic goals over a period of time." Services "should include parents, and **may involve technology-assisted intervention**." Examples: early intensive behavioural intervention, TEACCH, ESDM. Focused interventions address a single or limited range of skills. — [AAP 2020 full text](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] On ABA: "Most evidence-based treatment models are based on principles of ABA." Comprehensive ABA for younger children (EIBI) "is supported by a few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and a substantial single-subject literature". "Children younger than 12 years receiving more hours per week of ABA were found to be more likely to achieve the individualized goals." — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] On NDBIs: they "incorporate elements of ABA and developmental principles... with delivery of interventions in the context of naturally occurring social activities within natural environments. They use child-initiated teaching episodes, naturally occurring opportunities for learning, and turn-taking interactions within play routines". "The most extensively studied NDBI approach is the ESDM". In a multisite ESDM trial, "early age at entry to therapy and more hours of total therapy were associated with improved outcome." (The report's reference list also covers pivotal response training and JASPER-type joint-engagement interventions.) — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] On parent-mediated intervention: "Increasing evidence reveals that focused interventions delivered by trained parents or other caregivers can be an important part of a therapeutic program." "**More RCTs have been published on parent-mediated therapies than on other nonpharmacologic interventions.**" Parent-mediated interventions "are technique-focused and provide direct benefit to the child... and may be built on ABA approaches in natural settings." — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] Table 9 lists the common characteristics of empirically supported interventions: systematically assess skills; include family input (shared decision-making); "Select individualized measurable goals"; "Use assessment-based, empirically supported instructional methods to build, **generalize**, and maintain skills"; address core social-communication symptoms; properly trained providers with fidelity; "Ensure that multiple providers work collaboratively"; "Make use of the child's interests and preferences in determining reinforcement systems"; "Incorporate preferred activities to increase engagement." The report says it "is always accurate to describe the common characteristics of empirically supported interventions and recommend that families seek interventions that incorporate these features." — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] **On AAC (key quote)**: "When children do not spontaneously speak, augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) may be introduced... sign language, the Picture Exchange Communication System, and speech-generating devices. The use of AAC may help promote social interaction and understanding of the purpose of communication and **does not delay onset of speech. Indeed, it may enhance emergence of spoken words by pairing nonverbal and verbal communication.**" — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] On tablets: "Use of speech-generating devices and programs that use AAC on digital tablets also are increasing. These devices provide acoustic feedback to the child, and touch-screen tablets are relatively inexpensive and portable... **It cannot be assumed that the use of AAC alone will lead to functional oral communication without a therapeutic plan.**" Facilitated communication is not supported. — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] On video/computer interventions (social skills, older children): "Video- and computer-based social skill interventions may extend access to intervention **once an evidence base is established**." In social-skills training RCTs (ages 6-21), "Transfer of skills to other settings was inconsistent", and "child-mediated interventions taught separately from social settings have not had consistently beneficial effects". This is why interventions moved into real social settings with peers. — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] On sleep: "Children who play video games or engage in other screen time close to bedtime have later bedtimes and may have more difficulty falling asleep." — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)

**National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice (NCAEP), 2020 report (Steinbrenner et al., 2020; UNC Frank Porter Graham)**
- [GUIDELINE / systematic review] Per the AFIRM module (updated 2025), TAII "is a focused intervention that meets the evidence-based practice criteria with 17 single case design and 26 group design studies". It "has been effective for early intervention (0-2 years), preschoolers (3-5 years)" and older ages. Target outcomes include "academic/pre-academic, adaptive/self-help, behavior, cognitive, **communication, joint attention**, mental health, motor, play, school readiness, and social." — [AFIRM TAII Brief Packet, Hedges & AFIRM Team, updated 2025](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Technology-Aided-Instruction-Intervention-Brief-Packet-Hedges-AFIRM-Team-Updated-2025.pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] NCAEP EBP criteria: at least 2 randomised/quasi-experimental group studies, or at least 5 single-case studies, or a combination. Technology is defined as "any electronic item, equipment, application, or virtual network that is used intentionally to increase/maintain, and/or improve daily living, work/productivity, and recreation/leisure capabilities." "Parents and family members also can use Technology-Aided Instruction & Intervention in the home." — [AFIRM TAII brief](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Technology-Aided-Instruction-Intervention-Brief-Packet-Hedges-AFIRM-Team-Updated-2025.pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] The AFIRM TAII planning checklist asks whether the learner has the fine-motor ability to use a touchscreen independently and recommends discussing technology preferences with families and "the learner's behavior using technology at home." It defines generalisation as "when the target skill or behavior continues to occur when the intervention ends, in multiple settings, and with multiple individuals." — [AFIRM TAII brief](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Technology-Aided-Instruction-Intervention-Brief-Packet-Hedges-AFIRM-Team-Updated-2025.pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] TxAutism's summary of NCAEP shows TAII effective for ages 0-5 for academic/pre-academic, cognitive and communication outcomes. — [Texas Statewide Leadership for Autism, TAII](https://www.txautism.net/interventions/technology-aided-instruction-and-intervention)

**App-based interventions (research, limited)**
- [RESEARCH / SECONDARY] Coverage of the TOBY app (an iPad early-intervention app from Australia) describes it as not a replacement for behavioural therapy but a possible bridge for families on waiting lists, with small studies suggesting benefit mainly as an add-on. — [Spectrum/The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/children-autism-tablet-app-may-deliver-life-skills/); [Telethon Kids Institute report](https://www.thekids.org.au/our-research/reports-and-findings/2019/may-2019/parental-experiences-using-the-therapy-outcome)

### Inferences
- In paediatric terms, an app is best framed as one tool inside a parent-mediated, NDBI-style routine. The parent and the real-world interaction remain the core "active ingredient"; the app is a way to deliver structured practice. The AAP report explicitly lists "technology-assisted intervention" as something CTMs "may involve". It also warns that tools alone (even AAC) do not produce functional communication "without a therapeutic plan".
- TAII's EBP status does not mean that *any* app works. The NCAEP studies used technology within planned, systematic instruction with measured outcomes. A home-made "who" app fits that model only if it has clear goals, data on progress, and planned generalisation.
- The AAP's generalisation concern (inconsistent transfer from computer/social-skills training to real settings) applies directly. Using the child's own family photos is a reasonable step toward generalisation, but real-life practice ("Who is eating?" at the dinner table) is still needed.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether the AAP 2020 clinical report has been formally reaffirmed or superseded as of 2026; I found no newer AAP autism-management clinical report.
- The NCAEP 2020 report PDF did not download (the URL returned HTML). TAII figures come from the 2025 AFIRM module that cites it.
- I found no RCT of an app specifically teaching WH-questions to autistic preschoolers.
- I could not retrieve the AAP report's exact wording on JASPER/PRT, and the oft-cited "25 hours per week" intensity figure (from the 2001 National Research Council report) does not appear in the 2020 AAP text I searched.

## 4. Concerns clinicians raise: displacing human interaction, screen time and language delay, "virtual autism", generalisation, rewards, sensory overload

### Takeaway
The strongest, best-supported concerns are **displacement and "technoference"** (screens reduce parent-child talk), the **video deficit / transfer deficit** (young children learn less from screens than from people), and **solo, non-educational use** linked to language delay. Links between screen time and autism itself are **associational and contested**. A 2023 meta-analysis found the association not robust after correcting for publication bias, and "virtual autism" is not a diagnosis. Autism researchers in 2026 argue the term should be replaced with "screen-related developmental delay."

### Cited Findings
**Screen use and language**
- [RESEARCH] Madigan, McArthur, Anhorn, Eirich & Christakis (2020), *JAMA Pediatrics* 174(7):665-675: meta-analysis of 42 studies (18,905 children). Higher screen time was associated with poorer language (r = −0.14), and background TV more so (r = −0.19). **Co-viewing (r = 0.16)** and **educational programs (r = 0.13)** were associated with *better* language, as was later age of onset (r = 0.17). The authors say the findings support guidance to limit screens, choose high-quality programming and co-view. Effects are small and correlational. — [PubMed 32202633](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32202633/); [PMC7091394](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7091394)
- [RESEARCH] Brushe et al. (2024), *JAMA Pediatrics*: Australian cohort of 220 families using LENA recordings at 12-36 months. "Increases in screen time were associated with decreases in measures of parent-child talk" (adult words, child vocalisations, conversational turns), with the largest decreases at 36 months. This supports the "technoference" concept. — [PubMed 38436942](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38436942/)
- [RESEARCH] Strouse & Samson (2021), *Child Development*: meta-analysis of the **video deficit** (59 reports, 122 effect sizes, ages 0-6). Children learned less from video than face-to-face, by about half a standard deviation on average. The deficit **decreased with age**, and live vs. prerecorded video made no difference. The authors flag possible publication bias. — [PubMed 33491209](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33491209/)
- [GUIDELINE] CPS 2023: toddlers "still have difficulty transferring what they see from screens to real life"; preschoolers learn language "best from live, direct, and dynamic interactions." — [CPS 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10186096)

**Screen time and autism / "virtual autism"**
- [RESEARCH] Kushima et al. (2022), *JAMA Pediatrics* (Japan Environment and Children's Study, 84,030 mother-child dyads): among **boys only**, longer screen time at age 1 was associated with an ASD diagnosis at age 3. Adjusted ORs vs. no screen: <1 h 1.38 (NS); 1 to <2 h 2.16; 2 to <4 h 3.48; ≥4 h 3.02. There was no association in girls. This is observational and cannot show causation. — [PMC8804971](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8804971/); [PubMed 35099540](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35099540/)
- [RESEARCH] Ophir et al. (2023), *JAMA Network Open*: systematic review and meta-analysis of 46 observational studies (562,131 participants). The pooled association was positive (log OR 0.54), but after trim-and-fill correction for publication bias it fell to 0.22 (95% CI −0.004 to 0.44, **not significant**). The authors conclude the claimed link is "not sufficiently supported". They raise **reverse causation** (autistic children may gravitate to screens) and confounding. — [PMC10709772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10709772/)
- [RESEARCH, commentary] Krijnen, Scheeren, van Asselt, Begeer & Plak (2026), *Autism*, "Why the Term 'Virtual Autism' Warrants Caution". The term reinforces "outdated ideas about parental blame," misrepresents autism and confuses the public. The authors propose "**screen-related developmental delay**." — [PubMed 41871527 / DOI 10.1177/13623613261434478](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613261434478); [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A4301065/view)
- [RESEARCH, contrasting view] Ozyazici (2026), *Acta Psychologica*, reviews "virtual autism" more sympathetically. It recommends limiting and delaying screens and encouraging face-to-face play, while noting the term is "proposed" rather than established. — [DOI 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107285](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107285)
- [RESEARCH, pilot] Heffler et al. (2022), *Pediatrics International*: pilot with **9** autistic children aged 18-40 months who watched ≥2 h/day. A parent-education and weekly home-support program replaced screen time with social-engagement time over 6 months. Viewing fell from 5.6 h/day to 5 min/day, with significant improvements in core autism symptoms and parental stress. There was no control group, and the authors call the results "promising preliminary" findings. — [PubMed 36348519 / DOI 10.1111/ped.15343](https://doi.org/10.1111/ped.15343)

**Sensory processing**
- [RESEARCH] Heffler et al. (2024), *JAMA Pediatrics* (published 8 Jan 2024), "Early-Life Digital Media Experiences and Development of Atypical Sensory Processing": 1,471 children in the US National Children's Study. Any screen exposure by 12 months (vs. none) was associated with a 105% greater likelihood of "high" sensory behaviours at 33 months. The authors say causation cannot be concluded. — [Drexel University news release](https://drexel.edu/medicine/news-events/news-archive/2024/January/Putting-Toddler-In-Front-of-TV-Atypical-Sensory/); [Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20240123/early-screen-exposure-associated-with-atypical-sensory-processing)
- [CLINICIAN OPINION, AAP-published] Radesky: if a show or app makes a child "more active, aggressive, or self-stimulatory," remove it from rotation. — [HealthyChildren.org ASD media article](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/tips-tools/ask-the-pediatrician/Pages/How-should-I-handle-my-child%e2%80%99s-media-use-autism-spectrum-disorder.aspx)

**Rewards / engagement design**
- [GUIDELINE] AAP 2026 policy statement criticises "behavioral reinforcers" and other engagement-based features that "may prolong use and undermine child agency." — [Contemporary Pediatrics summary](https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/aap-calls-for-system-level-changes-to-improve-children-s-digital-media-environments)
- [CLINICIAN OPINION, AAP-published] Radesky: auto-advance features "are specifically designed to keep kids playing longer". She also notes "A few studies suggest children with ASD develop more excessive media use habits during childhood." — [HealthyChildren.org ASD media article](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/tips-tools/ask-the-pediatrician/Pages/How-should-I-handle-my-child%e2%80%99s-media-use-autism-spectrum-disorder.aspx)
- [GUIDELINE] By contrast, the AAP 2020 autism report endorses using "the child's interests and preferences in determining reinforcement systems" in intervention. Reinforcement for learning is standard practice; what the AAP criticises is engagement-maximising design. — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)

**Generalisation**
- [GUIDELINE] AAP 2020: transfer of skills from separately taught (incl. video/computer-based) social-skills training to other settings "was inconsistent". — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)

### Inferences
- A balanced "paediatrician's view" for the report: screens do not *cause* autism on current evidence, and parents should not be blamed. But heavy, solo, early screen use is associated with weaker language and less parent-child talk, and these risks matter more for a child who already has social-communication delays.
- The clinical tension around "celebrating correct answers" is a matter of design. A brief, human-delivered reinforcer for a correct answer within a short session is consistent with ABA/NDBI practice. Escalating animations, streaks, loot-style rewards or endless "one more" loops match the engagement-based design the AAP 2026 statement warns against.
- The video-deficit findings (smaller deficit with age; family photos are more familiar than generic images) suggest a personalised app with a parent present is better placed than generic video content. Still, the deficit is a reason to treat the app as a rehearsal aid and not as the main teaching context.

### Gaps
- I found no study of whether *interactive, parent-co-used, personalised* apps carry the same language risks as passive TV/video. Most screen-language research measures passive viewing or total screen time.
- Evidence that heavy screen exposure *causes* autism-like presentations that reverse with reduced screens rests largely on case reports and one uncontrolled 9-child pilot. No RCT was found.
- I found no paediatric-body statement specifically on sensory overload from apps beyond general advice (Radesky).

## 5. Practical recommendations a developmental paediatrician would give a parent using such an app

### Takeaway
Drawing on the guidelines and AAP clinician advice, the paediatric advice would be: confirm the child's age is appropriate for any screen use (≥2 years, or video-call-like shared use only if younger); keep sessions short (≈5-20 minutes, never more than the IAP's 20-30 min) and inside the ≤1 h/day total; **always use it together**; then immediately practise the same "who" questions with real people and real photos; avoid engagement hooks; track progress against measurable goals; coordinate with the child's SLP/BCBA/OT/developmental paediatrician; embrace AAC; and seek or keep up professional evaluation. The app should not replace evidence-based therapy.

### Cited Findings
**Age and amount**
- [GUIDELINE] Under 2: no screens except video-chatting (AAP 2016, CPS 2023, WHO 2019); IAP allows only "minimal and occasional" video calls with distant family. 2-5: ≤1 h/day. IAP: "each session not more than 20-30 min; the lesser, the better". Indian Psychiatric Society: ≤30 min per session, ≤2 sessions/day, for "educational games or teaching aid" under supervision. — [IAP 2022](https://www.indianpediatrics.net/mar2022/235.pdf); [CPS 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10186096); [WHO 2019](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more); [UW summary of AAP 2016](https://sph.washington.edu/news-events/news/updated-digital-media-guidelines-address-growing-impact-child-health)
- [GUIDELINE] No screens during meals, within an hour of bedtime, or as a calming tool. — [IAP 2022](https://www.indianpediatrics.net/mar2022/235.pdf); [CPS 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10186096); [AAP 5 Cs toddlers](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/kids-and-screen-time-5-cs-questions-for-toddlers-and-preschoolers.aspx)

**Co-play and design**
- [GUIDELINE] Always supervised and shared (IAP). Co-view/co-use (CPS, AAP). Joint media engagement is associated with better learning (AAP 2026). Use a shared family device and avoid open access to a tablet (AAP 2026 explainer; 5 Cs; IAP). — [IAP 2022](https://www.indianpediatrics.net/mar2022/235.pdf); [AAP policy explained](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/helping-kids-thrive-in-a-digital-world-AAP-policy-explained.aspx); [5 Cs toddlers](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/kids-and-screen-time-5-cs-questions-for-toddlers-and-preschoolers.aspx)
- [CLINICIAN OPINION, AAP-published] For autistic children, make media "a shared experience, rather than something kids do alone"; take turns; use visual timers; avoid auto-advance; switch away from content a child gets "stuck" on or that increases self-stimulatory behaviour. — [Radesky, HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/tips-tools/ask-the-pediatrician/Pages/How-should-I-handle-my-child%e2%80%99s-media-use-autism-spectrum-disorder.aspx)
- [GUIDELINE] Avoid engagement-based features (autoplay, endless scroll, behavioural reinforcers that prolong use). — [AAP 2026 via Contemporary Pediatrics](https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/aap-calls-for-system-level-changes-to-improve-children-s-digital-media-environments)

**Bridge to real life / generalisation**
- [GUIDELINE] Toddlers struggle to transfer from screens to real life; language is learned best in "live, direct, and dynamic interactions." — [CPS 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10186096)
- [GUIDELINE] Effective interventions "build, generalize, and maintain skills". NDBIs teach within "naturally occurring social activities within natural environments." — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] AAP 5 Cs: "If your child is watching something interesting, talk about it." Set aside daily time to play, read or go outside. — [5 Cs toddlers](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/kids-and-screen-time-5-cs-questions-for-toddlers-and-preschoolers.aspx)
- [GUIDELINE] WHO: when sedentary, reading and storytelling with a caregiver is encouraged. — [WHO 2019](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more)

**Coordinate with professionals; measure progress**
- [GUIDELINE] "Ensure that multiple providers work collaboratively"; "Select individualized measurable goals... on the basis of objective assessment"; shared decision-making with families. — [AAP 2020 Table 9](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] Parent-mediated interventions have the largest RCT base among non-drug options and "can be an important part of a therapeutic program". This supports parents learning techniques from therapists and carrying them into home routines (including an app). — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- [RESEARCH] Parent report on Vineland of "who/why" question production was highly accurate. Parent tracking of this skill is credible. — [Jyotishi et al. 2017 RIDD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5266545)
- [GUIDELINE] The AFIRM TAII process includes checking touchscreen motor ability, discussing technology habits with families, and planning generalisation across settings and people. — [AFIRM TAII brief](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Technology-Aided-Instruction-Intervention-Brief-Packet-Hedges-AFIRM-Team-Updated-2025.pdf)

**AAC**
- [GUIDELINE] AAC "does not delay onset of speech" and "may enhance emergence of spoken words", but AAC on tablets needs "a therapeutic plan." — [AAP 2020](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)

**When to seek help**
- [GUIDELINE] AAP: ASD-specific screening at 18 and 24 months plus surveillance; ASD can be diagnosed from 18 months, and evidence-based interventions exist. — [AAP 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31843864/)
- [GUIDELINE] CDC milestones are what ≥75% of children do by an age, so missing one is a reason to talk to the doctor and "act early." Example: by 3 years, asking who/what/where/why questions and naming actions in pictures. — [CDC 3-year milestones](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)
- [RESEARCH, pilot] For a young autistic child with heavy screen use, a parent program that replaced screens with social engagement was well tolerated and associated with symptom improvement (uncontrolled n=9 pilot). — [Heffler et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/ped.15343)

### Inferences
- A developmental paediatrician would likely tell the parent the following. (1) Your idea fits guidance best as a **shared, short, purposeful** activity, roughly a "teaching aid" in IAP/Indian Psychiatric Society terms, not entertainment. (2) Make every app session end in real life, for example asking "Who is eating?" at a family meal and pointing to the actual person. The real-life ask is the goal; the app is rehearsal. (3) Share the app and its data with the child's SLP/BCBA so targets match therapy goals. (4) Don't let the app crowd out therapy hours, play or talk. (5) If the child does not use speech, pairing the app with AAC is fine and will not stop speech. These are syntheses of the cited guidance, not quotes from one paediatrician.
- Sensible progress markers include: accuracy with the 2-choice task across different photos of the same people; accuracy with new photos; answering "who" about real people in the room; answering with a name or AAC symbol instead of only tapping; and using "who" spontaneously. These go beyond the sources and should be presented as clinical-logic suggestions.
- The child's age matters a lot. If he is under 2, all guideline bodies would advise against an app and toward the same game played with real photo albums and people.

### Gaps
- No professional body specifies an "ideal" session length for *therapeutic/educational* app use by autistic children. The 20-30 min (IAP) and ≤30 min × 2 (Indian Psychiatric Society) figures are general-population or general-educational limits.
- I found no clinical guideline on how to measure progress for app-delivered WH-question practice specifically.

## 6. Published interviews, articles and quotes from developmental paediatricians and SLPs (AAP HealthyChildren, ASHA, Child Mind Institute, Autism Speaks, Spectrum/The Transmitter)

### Takeaway
The most authoritative clinician-voice sources are AAP parent articles by developmental-behavioural paediatricians **Jenny Radesky** (autism and media, 2023) and **Tiffany Munzer** (2026 policy explainer). SLP-literature commentary in 2024-2025 mainly concerns the gestalt-language-processing debate. I found no published interview with a developmental paediatrician specifically about apps for teaching "who" questions.

### Cited Findings
- [CLINICIAN OPINION, AAP] Jenny Radesky, MD, FAAP (Professor of Pediatrics, University of Michigan; Co-Medical Director, AAP Center of Excellence on Social Media and Youth Mental Health): media for autistic children should be "a shared experience, rather than something kids do alone." Watch for auto-advance features "designed to keep kids playing longer." "No apps or games are proven to help attention span." Use favourite characters to teach skills such as recognising emotions. — [HealthyChildren.org, updated 29 Mar 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/tips-tools/ask-the-pediatrician/Pages/How-should-I-handle-my-child%e2%80%99s-media-use-autism-spectrum-disorder.aspx)
- [CLINICIAN OPINION, AAP] Tiffany Munzer, MD, FAAP (developmental-behavioural paediatrician, University of Michigan C.S. Mott Children's Hospital; lead author of the 2026 AAP policy): families should "move beyond simple screen-time limits and focus on quality, context and conversation"; "Watch and play alongside your kids." — [HealthyChildren.org AAP policy explained, Jun 2026](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/helping-kids-thrive-in-a-digital-world-AAP-policy-explained.aspx); [Healio video on 5 Cs (Munzer)](https://cm.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20240510/video-the-5-cs-of-media-use-for-children)
